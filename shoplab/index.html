Who is your AI agent buying for?

At the Rotman Behavioural Research Lab, shoppers and AI models chose from the same catalogue using each shopper’s own preferences. The purchase decisions were non-incentivized. Participants spent no money and received no products.

Shoppers
98
Shopping tasks
582
Product categories

How the experiment worked

State a preference

Before each category, shoppers described what they wanted in their own words. They could specify a firm budget, a comfortable price range, brands and intended uses.

Shop independently

People browsed eight items per page and could choose none. Two randomly assigned models each received the whole shuffled catalogue in one request, including individual photos, specifications, prices, stars, rating counts and badges.

Reconsider the choice

At the end, shoppers compared suggestions with their initial picks. The model identities were hidden, a placebo was included, and shoppers could keep their pick, switch, or choose none.

Search and position

Shoppers chose near the top. AI picks came from deeper in the catalogue.

The first page held 45.1% of human picks and 10.5% of AI picks in matched comparisons. The median product sat at position 12 for shoppers and 44 for models.

The catalogue was shuffled separately for each task. Shoppers had to open another page to see more items, while models received them all at once. Even on tasks where shoppers never sorted, the first-page shares were 49.6% and 11.1%.

Product discovery

What did the suggestions bring into view?

Shoppers opened a median of two results pages. In 231 of 582 tasks, they stayed on page one.

Shoppers switched from their original product to an AI suggestion 95 times. In 74 of those 95 switches (77.9%), the new product had never appeared on a results page they opened before choosing. This measure follows the pages they actually opened, including after sorting.

Model agreement

Two models often chose the exact same product.

With up to 100 products to choose from, the two models picked the same item for the same shopper in 313 of 571 tasks (54.8%). Pairing their picks across different shoppers in the same category yielded just 4.7% agreement.

Personal taste

Beauty is in the eye of the shopper.

In 153 tasks, an AI suggestion cost no more, had at least as many stars and ratings, and improved on at least one of the three. Shoppers still kept their own pick in 86. In 26 of those 86 (30.2%), they praised their pick’s looks or criticized the appearance of an improving suggestion.

How the study and comparisons work

The catalogue. Headphones, sneakers, backpacks, water bottles and toothpaste each had 100 products. Fitness watches had 77. Category order and product order varied across participants. We also varied displayed prices, stars and rating counts, and assigned Sponsored and Overall Pick badges. Some shoppers had access to sorting. A model received the same task-specific offers in the original order regardless of how the shopper later browsed.

The position curves. We match each valid model pick to the human’s initial product choice on that exact task. The pooled view contains 1,093 model–shopper pairs from 552 tasks. A task counts twice when both models returned a valid pick, and once when only one did. The 30 initial no-purchase decisions have no product position. Filtering by model keeps only that model’s tasks for both curves. The chart includes all eligible pages, with four items on the last page of a 100-item catalogue and five on the last watch page.

What position tells us. The curves show a strong concentration of human choices near the top of the randomized catalogue. Browsing takes effort, and Sponsored badges appeared more often on the first page. The curves alone cannot isolate the effect of position. In an actual purchase with money at stake, people might search differently.

Model agreement. Each task assigned two of Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 3.8 Flash. Both returned a valid product in 571 of 582 tasks. For the different-shopper benchmark, we paired one model’s pick with each of the other model’s picks for different shoppers in the same category, keeping the model pair fixed. We weighted each category and pair by its number of valid tasks. Both preferences and the displayed offers varied across shoppers, so this comparison combines those differences.

Personal taste. We compared the actual price, star rating and number of ratings displayed in each task. We counted each task once, even if two suggestions qualified. Of the 86 decisions to keep the original, 17 explanations favored its looks and nine more identified a visual objection to a qualifying AI suggestion. General references to appearance and comments about a different suggestion fall outside the 26. These are shoppers’ reported reasons. The experiment did not randomize product appearance.
