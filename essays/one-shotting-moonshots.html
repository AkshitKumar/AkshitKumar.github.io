What Is the Value of One-Shotting Moonshots?

Akshit Kumar · October 27, 2026

Read the technical paper (PDF) →

I have been thinking about OpenAI’s release of more than 700 mathematical manuscripts earlier this month, and the discomfort it has generated alongside the excitement. These manuscripts make claims with different levels of verification, so there is plenty of work to do before treating every announced advance as settled. But even setting correctness aside, there is a question here that I find difficult to dismiss. What does society gain when we receive a solution without much of the understanding that would ordinarily develop around finding it?

A difficult research problem gives people a reason to explore. We try approaches that seem natural, discover why they fail, borrow ideas from another field, and occasionally stumble upon something useful for an entirely different question. Some of those discoveries survive even when the original problem remains unsolved. Perhaps, in a fairly literal sense, the real treasure is the connections made along the way.

We communicate some of this through papers, but also through seminars, workshops, conference conversations, and discussions with students. A finished proof can look inevitable. Hearing its author explain where they got stuck, what they first believed, and which observation changed their mind can reveal a much larger set of ideas. Over time, these exchanges become part of the knowledge that other researchers draw upon.

AI may explore many routes internally and then communicate only the successful one. We receive a way of getting from the starting point to the answer, with little indication of the surrounding territory. A person using AI can face the same issue. They may carefully verify each step and understand how the argument works, while still learning relatively little about the other approaches that the problem might have inspired.

This is part of what makes Terence Tao’s discussion of “proof indigestion” so pertinent. Producing results, checking them, explaining them, and absorbing their ideas into a community’s understanding are all substantial activities. Making the first much faster puts pressure on the others.

Breakthroughs and the knowledge around them

Yet I hesitate when this concern becomes a general argument for preserving the slower process of human discovery. Imagine that AI helps discover a treatment that, after proper testing, proves safe and effective against a devastating disease. We would certainly want to understand its mechanism and learn from the discovery. But the benefit to patients would already be enormous. I would find it difficult to argue that we should have waited for humans to reach the same result through a more illuminating journey.

Different communities may reasonably weigh these benefits differently. Even within mathematics, people disagree about the relative importance of solving problems and developing conceptual understanding. Timothy Gowers’s discussion of these differences helped me think about why people who care deeply about the same subject can respond so differently to AI.

A map and a path

I tried to give this tension a simple formal treatment in On the Value of One-Shotting Moonshots. Imagine ideas arranged on a grid. Researchers build outward from what they already know, attempting to establish connections to neighboring ideas. Both humans and AI can investigate many possibilities, encounter difficulties, and try again. AI has an advantage in establishing those connections and can therefore reach further.

The comparison is between two deliberately extreme ways of communicating this research. The human community shares the map it has explored. AI shares a successful path to its deepest result. The path itself contains useful knowledge, but much of AI’s surrounding exploration remains private. This is generous to human research, which certainly does not communicate everything it discovers. It also gives AI a demanding benchmark by limiting its contribution to one report.

Society receives two kinds of benefit. There is the value of the breakthrough itself, and there is the value of the ideas made available for other people to use. The struggle matters through the understanding and possibilities it leaves behind.

In a benchmark where the value of deeper breakthroughs grows at the same rate as the value of a broader public map, the model gives a simple threshold. An AI that is somewhat better at discovery can still contribute less overall than a slower research community that communicates broadly. As its capability advantage becomes sufficiently large, the value of what it discovers can compensate for the narrower knowledge it shares. Giving more weight to public knowledge raises that threshold.

The assumptions behind this comparison matter. The broader model allows different conclusions depending on how much more valuable distant discoveries become and whether AI merely speeds up research or also makes increasingly difficult problems relatively easier. My hope is that this gives us a vocabulary for the disagreement. Someone who sees the current rush of AI papers as pernicious may place a very high value on the surrounding understanding. Someone excited about discoveries far beyond our present reach may see enormous value in the results themselves.

Turning results into understanding

Actual research will, of course, live somewhere between these extremes. People can use AI to reach further and then explore the new territory around the result. A breakthrough might even give us a starting point from which we can ask questions that would previously have been out of reach. There is a hopeful possibility here that deserves as much attention as the concern about what gets lost.

This is why I found OpenAI’s latest announcement about working with academic communities encouraging. It describes the manuscripts as “raw research data” and experiments with different arrangements for turning them into scientific understanding. It also explicitly recognizes the contribution of researchers who explain and contextualize the work.

Ryan Williams’s thread makes this more concrete. He describes joining Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi to organize an open, independent examination of a claimed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis. They requested public access to the underlying materials and support for a conference involving the wider community. That seems like a promising way to bring scrutiny, explanation, and further research into the process.

The problem of scale

What I keep coming back to, though, is scale. Organizing expert attention around a few prominent discoveries seems feasible. What happens when thousands of people can produce plausible research manuscripts with a few prompts? The ability to generate these papers can spread much faster than the expertise needed to assess and explain them. Even if only a fraction contain useful advances, they could demand enormous attention from a relatively small community.

I am glad that more people may be able to contribute to research. But wider participation in generating proposed discoveries also creates a collective problem of deciding what deserves attention. Some valuable ideas may remain buried in papers that nobody has time to understand. AI could help with explanation and synthesis too. How much that expands our capacity to absorb research is an important part of the question.

Who gets credit for making research useful?

There is also an incentive problem. Suppose a young researcher spends several months extracting a useful technique from a difficult AI argument, finding its limitations, and writing something that others can learn from. How will that contribution be recognized? Will journals, hiring committees, and funding agencies value it enough to make the effort worthwhile? It seems optimistic to expect a growing amount of this work if announcing a result attracts substantially more recognition than making it understandable.

OpenAI’s announcement acknowledges recognition and commits support for work around the results. Those are welcome steps. I would like to think further about arrangements that could work at a much larger scale. Perhaps we need dedicated funding for explanation and synthesis, clearer ways to attribute these contributions, or publication formats that make them more visible. Each possibility brings questions about quality, independence, and how scarce attention should be allocated.

These seem like interesting questions to mull over, model, and try to find workable solutions to. How do we support people who turn a difficult argument into something others can use? How do we encourage exploration around a breakthrough when the headline result has already been claimed? And how can AI help with that work as well as with producing the next discovery?

I would like AI to help us reach places we could never have reached on our own. I also hope we leave enough of a map for someone else to wander off in a direction none of us had thought to explore.
